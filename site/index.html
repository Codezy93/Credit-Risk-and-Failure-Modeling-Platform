PROBLEM DEFINITION

A credit-risk score is only one part of the system.

The project starts with a practical question: can information available around loan origination help rank the risk of a later charge-off? The second question is operational: can the same saved model process replayed applications, expose its behavior, and recover its results after a failure?

LendingClub's accepted-loan file gives this project enough scale for SQL analysis and a realistic modelling workflow. It also creates important constraints. Accepted loans are a selected population, outstanding loans have unresolved outcomes, and a downloaded snapshot is not the same thing as a point-in-time lending system.

The scope I kept

Build a retrospective classifier for Charged Off vs Fully Paid among resolved, 36-month accepted loans. Keep experiments, explanations, loss proxies, and infrastructure evidence distinct. Do not describe the result as an approved lending policy or a regulatory default model.

CLEANING

Make invalid values explicit. Keep unusual values visible.

The cleaning SQL parses IDs and numeric values with try_cast, parses month strings explicitly, removes the percent sign from interest rates, and extracts numeric loan terms. Employment duration is encoded with 0 for “under one year” and 10 for “10+ years”; neither is an exact duration at the boundary.

Nonpositive income, negative DTI or utilization, and credit-history dates after issue date become missing. A high but parseable value is treated differently from an impossible one: utilization above 100% is retained, and extreme income or DTI values remain available for investigation rather than being silently capped.

BIVARIATE & MULTIVARIATE ANALYSIS

A useful pattern still needs a denominator.

Next, outcome rates are examined across categories and numeric bands. Home ownership, purpose, loan amount, credit score and affordability measures show different observed charged-off shares. Every rate needs its group count: a small, unusual category can look dramatic without providing stable evidence.

Missingness also carries information. In ordinary resolved development loans, missing employment has a {{employment_missing_rate}}% charged-off share versus {{employment_present_rate}}% when employment is present. That is a reason to preserve and inspect missingness, not to replace it with zero or call it random.

DISCRIMINATION, CALIBRATION & POLICY

AUC is a ranking metric, not the whole decision.

Evaluation separates ranking, probability quality and threshold behavior. ROC-AUC, Gini, KS and average precision describe discrimination. Brier score, reliability bins and calibration error describe probability behavior. Precision, recall and the confusion matrix depend on the chosen flagging threshold.

The original F1 policy was selected on 2014 at threshold {{threshold}}. On 2015, it has precision {{test_precision}} and recall {{test_recall}}. At 0.5, the incumbent flags no loans in these cohorts. That is why a familiar threshold is not automatically a useful risk policy.

Class imbalance matters for precision and useful baselines, but it does not make a 0.65 AUC equivalent to random ranking. Probability calibration can still deteriorate as the observed charged-off rate changes over time. A single scalar score cannot summarize all three issues.

The logistic coefficient review found negative adjusted signs for delinquency and bankruptcies despite positive univariate relationships, with signs changing across years. Those are conditional associations in this selected population. The logistic baseline is not presented as a monotonic WoE scorecard, and the signs are not interpreted as protective causal effects.

Try different models without moving the goalposts.

XGBoost experiments separate feature scope from model choice. One group uses the original baseline inputs, another adds bureau candidates, and a third includes underwriting outputs such as grade and interest rate. The last group answers a later scoring-point question and is labelled accordingly.

The corrected stopping protocol fits the stopping model on 2011–2012, chooses tree budgets on 2013, then refits on 2011–2013 with those budgets frozen. The 2014 cohort compares candidates; it does not also choose the early-stopping point. This closes a reuse mechanism without turning previously explored data into a fresh holdout.

Neural-network experiments test two small dense classifiers, including a 64→32 architecture, plus separate loss-model challengers. Their epoch budget follows the temporal stopping/refit pattern. The best ANN does not outperform the incumbent on the same original-input validation comparison.

[11]risk

LOSS MODELLING

Separate the probability score from exposure and severity.

The loss layer asks a different question: how much loss does a loan's score imply when combined with exposure and loss severity? Snapshot residual principal and net recoveries provide retrospective proxies, with validity checks and a separate defaulted-loan scope.

Expected loss proxyscore × estimated LGD × estimated EAD

EAD is based on funded amount minus principal received. LGD uses net recoveries relative to residual exposure, on valid charged-off rows. Recovery and payment fields serve this separate loss-target scope; they do not become classifier predictors.

[13]stream

KAFKA & PYSPARK

Put the same model in an observable data flow.

The streaming stage replays prepared historical application features into Kafka. PySpark Structured Streaming parses messages, validates the feature contract, and scores Arrow batches on workers using the same saved sklearn pipeline. It does not train a Spark ML model or refit preprocessing.

PROCESSING PATHhistorical replay

  2. KafkaThree input partitions
  3. PySparkValidate & batch score
  4. Kafka outputsScores / invalid records

{{code_stream}}

The schema allows null values that the original pipeline can handle. Malformed JSON, missing feature keys, extra feature keys and invalid nonnull numeric values go to an invalid-record topic. Outcome fields inside the feature object are rejected. This is input-schema validation, not source certification or comprehensive economic-range validation.

The model file is distributed with SparkFiles and checked against its recorded SHA before the query starts. Outputs contain the event ID, source topic/partition/offset, model fingerprint, probability, frozen threshold and risk flag. They omit application features and original loan IDs.

Checkpoint state preserves completed source offsets. A scoring contract prevents accidentally resuming the same checkpoint with a different model, feature list, threshold or topic configuration. Kafka output remains at least once, so a retried micro-batch can still write repeated scores.

First check: scoring parity.

The initial finite replay checked 100 real loan scores against direct model predictions and routed three invalid fixtures correctly. A clean checkpoint restart consumed no additional committed records. That established correctness before measuring traffic.

Source: streaming contract and commands.

[15]fault

FAILURE RECOVERY

Stop the process while the producer keeps going.

The recovery experiment sends {{recovered_rows}} main events at {{replay_rate}}/second. It waits until Spark has written a source-offset file for a batch without its matching commit, then sends SIGKILL to the test's own Spark process group. After {{downtime}} seconds, it restarts Spark from the same checkpoint while the producer remains active.

This test exposed a real issue in Spark 4.1.2: Kafka progress reporting could crash while resuming an uncommitted batch. The optional runtime and matching connector were patched to 4.1.4 before the successful experiment. The core modelling environment and classifier artifact did not change.

[16]ops

DEDUPLICATION & ELK

Persist the result before committing the delivery.

The Kafka consumer stores one logical score per event_id in SQLite. A separate delivery ledger uses Kafka topic/partition/offset as its key. A repeated event with the same logical result is suppressed; a conflicting result for an existing event ID is recorded without overwriting the first score.

{{code_store}}

Scores, delivery records and a monitoring outbox commit in one SQLite transaction, using WAL and FULL synchronization. Kafka offsets advance afterwards. The outbox is flushed to structured logs before being marked exported. Re-exported monitoring records keep the same log_id, which Elasticsearch uses as its document ID.

A second fault closes the database/Kafka commit window: the store exits immediately after the SQLite commit, before Kafka offset commit. Restarting the same group and database retries the messages. The independent audit waits for both an empty outbox and zero committed consumer-group lag; outbox drainage alone is not enough.

Make the operational state inspectable.

JSON-lines logs feed Logstash's persistent file reader and queue. Elasticsearch stores typed monitoring events. The eight-panel Kibana dashboard shows stored results, suppressed duplicates, conflicts, p95 latency, results per minute, checkpoint lag, mean risk score and operational errors.

{{code_elk}}

The final experiment verified every expected monitoring ID against Elasticsearch. The local on-demand checker also reports conflicts, invalid outputs, high p95 latency and high checkpoint lag. A stalled-progress check is available when processing is expected to be running.

What is local, and what is not live here?

Kibana and Elasticsearch run locally; this public notebook does not connect to them. Threshold checks emit JSON on demand. Scheduled alerting and external notifications are not configured, and a changing score histogram is not presented as a validated statistical drift detector.

Source: storage consumer · monitoring setup · independent reconciliation.

CONCLUSIONS

The useful result is the complete chain of evidence.

The original classifier has modest discrimination. Expanded feature research improves validation ranking within a different, explicitly qualified scope. Neural networks do not automatically improve the result. Explanations reveal which inputs matter and where observed error rates differ, while the serving experiment shows that the frozen model can be replayed and its results recovered after interruption.

The operational work also changed the implementation: a real mid-batch restart exposed an upstream Spark bug; an independent consumer-offset audit tightened the recovery check. The final recorded experiment preserves every main score, suppresses the injected duplicates, flags a conflict and reconciles the monitoring logs.

SUPPORTED BY THIS PROJECT
  • SQL-led analysis of {{clean_rows}} cleaned accepted loans.
  • Documented temporal comparisons with train-only preprocessing.
  • Explainability and observed business-segment error audits.
  • Loss proxies with explicitly separate assumptions.
  • Measured local load and controlled process-failure recovery.
STILL OUTSIDE THE EVIDENCE
  • A fresh holdout or a point-in-time lending deployment.
  • Protected-group fairness or causal borrower effects.
  • Regulatory PD, downturn LGD or certified Basel capital.
  • Broker failover, disk-loss recovery or production SLAs.
  • Scheduled external alerts and a validated drift detector.

The final artifact connects each modelling decision to a source, a measurement, and a practical limitation.
